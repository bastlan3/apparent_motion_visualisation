Methods (McBeath & Shepard, 1989, Perception & Psychophysics 46(4), 333–337)

DisplayIBM XT, low-resolution graphics (p. 334). Resolution, refresh, luminance, colours, polarity not given. Fig. 1 shows black shapes and a black partition on white. Assumed here: black shapes and a partition of the same black on a light-grey field (as in Fig. 1), anti-aliased canvas.
Viewing distance / sizeApprox. 80 cm; maximum diameter of each shape about 1.5° (p. 334). All stimuli are drawn in degrees using the Display setup (px/cm, distance). Default 37.8 px/cm if not calibrated; default distance 80 cm = paper.
LayoutVertical partition in the middle; one shape alternates left and right of it; a gap (window) in the partition just large enough to admit the rigid passage of the shape (Fig. 1b). Both shapes placed closer to the bottom of the screen (p. 334-335). Partition width 0.3°, full-height partition, field 14° × 11.5°, line joining the shape centres 2.5° above the field bottom, no fixation mark: assumptions/unspecified. Window height = twice the shape's largest radius + 2 px (so any orientation fits).
ShapesNarrow (line drawing after Farrell, 1983: straight stick with a short perpendicular tick and a short hook at one end), Medium (silhouette resembling Farrell & Shepard, 1981 polygons: lumpy broad end tapering to one tip), Wide (Pac Man: circle with a pie-shaped segment removed; wedge about a quarter, 90°, in Fig. 1a-b) — Fig. 1a. Outlines are redrawn by eye from Fig. 1a; exact vertices unspecified. Wedge 90° total (from the figure). In Fig. 1a the standalone Wide shape has its wedge at upper left; in the trials the wedge is oriented so the shape is never symmetric about a vertical axis (p. 334); mouth at -45° then +90° rotation here.
TimingEach shape on for a fixed 550 ms; ISI 0, 50, 100, 150 or 200 ms, giving SOA 550–750 ms (p. 334). Durations are converted to whole frames at the measured refresh rate (see display line). Left shape shown first; the 550 ms shape-on and ISI blank alternate continuously until the trial ends; outline redraws of canvas, no fixation mark.
Separation of centres3°, 6° or 9° (p. 334–335).
Orientation difference90° or 180° between the two presented orientations (p. 334–335). Narrow and medium shapes are oriented so their axes of elongation pass through the centre of rotation specified by the kinematic geometry for the pair (p. 334). Rotation direction fixed (not counterbalanced); placement of the rotation centre per pair is my construction from this description.
Design / trial structure3 shapes × 3 separations × 2 angles × 5 ISIs × 2 replications = 180 trials per observer, random order; about 45 min (p. 335). 10 observers (p. 335). Order is fully shuffled here; no counterbalancing of side or direction; 500 ms blank before each trial; ratings accepted only after a 1.5 s minimum dwell.
Response: windowUsing a joystick, the observer adjusts the vertical height of the window in the partition to obtain the strongest illusion of smooth motion of the object back and forth through it. Measure: height (deg) of the window centre above the straight line joining the two shape centres (Fig. 1c; p. 334). Observers were asked to try for a placement at or above that line (p. 335). Joystick replaced by Up/Down keys or buttons at 1.9°/s; random start height in [0°, upper limit]; window travel limited to 7° above the line, with the lowest position being the straight line itself (window centre at 0°; paper p. 336: adjustable range is one in which only the lowest extreme corresponds to a straight path; the 7° upper limit is an assumption); window height is stored in degrees so a Display change mid-trial keeps its position.
Response: confidence3-point rating of confidence in the final placement: 3 = high confidence and well-defined path; 1 = low confidence and poorly defined path; 2 = intermediate (p. 335).
Instruction primingObservers were primed toward upward-curving paths by three means: both shapes placed low on the screen, the instruction to place the window at or above the straight line, and choosing the 90° trials with that geometry (pp. 334–335). All three are implemented as described.
Window gapPaper: just large enough to admit rigid passage of the shape (p. 334). Assumed here: gap height = 2 × the largest shape radius + 2 px, a conservative choice that fits any orientation.
Not specified in the paperResolution, refresh rate, luminance, colours and polarity; field size (here 14° × 11.5°); partition width (0.3°); joystick gain; start height; inter-trial interval; fixation; counterbalancing; exact outlines of the shapes (redrawn from Fig. 1a).
